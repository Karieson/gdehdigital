1. Introduction to Farming

Farming is the practice of cultivating crops and raising livestock to produce food, income, raw materials and other agricultural products. It supports household livelihoods, employment, food security and economic development.

Successful farming requires planning, knowledge, suitable resources, proper management and continuous learning. Farmers need to understand their land, soil, climate, water availability, crops, pests, markets and production costs.

Modern agriculture combines traditional farming knowledge with scientific research and digital technologies to improve productivity, reduce losses and protect natural resources.

Key principle: Good farming is not simply about planting crops. It involves managing the entire production process, from planning and land preparation to harvesting, storage and marketing.

3. Farm Planning

Farm planning is the process of deciding what to produce, where to produce it, when to plant, which resources are required and how the harvest will be sold or used.

3.1 Why farm planning is important

  • Helps farmers use available land efficiently.
  • Reduces unnecessary expenditure.
  • Supports timely purchase of seeds and inputs.
  • Helps estimate expected production and income.
  • Identifies water, labour and equipment requirements.
  • Helps farmers prepare for climate and market risks.

3.2 Important planning considerations

  1. Land: Determine the size, location, soil type and condition of the farm.
  2. Climate: Understand rainfall, temperature, wind and seasonal changes.
  3. Water: Identify reliable and safe sources of irrigation water.
  4. Crop choice: Select crops suitable for local conditions and market demand.
  5. Budget: Estimate costs of seeds, labour, equipment, transport and water.
  6. Market: Identify potential buyers before production.

4. Land Preparation

Land preparation involves making the soil suitable for planting and supporting healthy crop establishment. Good preparation improves seed placement, root development and field management.

4.1 Main land preparation activities

  • Remove harmful weeds and unwanted vegetation.
  • Collect rubbish, stones and unsuitable materials.
  • Prepare the soil through appropriate tillage.
  • Incorporate suitable organic matter where available.
  • Level or shape the land according to the irrigation method.
  • Prepare planting beds, ridges, furrows or basins where required.

4.2 Common land preparation methods

Manual preparation

Farmers use hand tools such as hoes, forks, spades and rakes. It is suitable for small farms and gardens.

Animal-powered preparation

Draught animals may pull suitable ploughing equipment to prepare fields where the terrain and soil conditions allow their use.

Mechanical preparation

Tractors and other agricultural machinery are used for larger farms. Equipment should be selected according to soil conditions and the intended crop.

Important: Excessive tillage can damage soil structure, increase erosion and reduce soil moisture. Choose the minimum suitable soil disturbance for the farming system.

5. Soil Management

Soil is a major natural resource in crop production. It provides plants with physical support, water, air and essential nutrients.

5.1 Main soil types

Sandy soil

Sandy soil has relatively large particles and drains quickly. It is generally easy to work but may lose water and nutrients rapidly.

Clay soil

Clay soil contains very small particles. It can retain considerable water and nutrients but may drain slowly and become difficult to work when wet.

Loam soil

Loam contains a balanced mixture of sand, silt and clay. Many crops grow well in suitable loam soils because they can retain moisture while allowing drainage.

5.2 Maintaining soil fertility

  • Apply compost and well-decomposed manure.
  • Use suitable fertilizers based on soil requirements.
  • Practice crop rotation.
  • Grow suitable legumes to support soil fertility.
  • Maintain crop residues where appropriate.
  • Prevent soil erosion and unnecessary disturbance.

5.3 Soil testing

Soil testing helps determine important properties such as soil pH, nutrient availability and salinity. Test results help farmers select appropriate soil amendments and fertilizer applications.

Soil should be tested using a representative sample. Where possible, seek guidance from an agricultural extension officer or an accredited soil laboratory.

6. Seeds and Crop Selection

Choosing suitable seeds is one of the most important decisions in crop production. Seed quality influences germination, crop establishment, maturity and yield.

6.1 Characteristics of good-quality seeds

  • High germination capacity.
  • Free from visible insect damage and disease.
  • Properly stored and labelled.
  • Suitable for the intended growing conditions.
  • Obtained from reliable and approved suppliers.
  • Appropriate maturity period for the production season.

6.2 Factors affecting crop selection

  • Local climate and seasonal rainfall.
  • Soil type and fertility.
  • Availability of irrigation water.
  • Resistance or tolerance to relevant pests and diseases.
  • Crop maturity period.
  • Local food preferences and market demand.
  • Expected production costs and selling prices.

7. Planting and Crop Spacing

Planting is the process of placing seeds or seedlings into prepared soil under suitable conditions. Correct planting supports uniform crop establishment.

7.1 Planting methods

  • Direct seeding: Seeds are planted directly in the field.
  • Transplanting: Seedlings are raised in a nursery and moved to the field.
  • Broadcasting: Seeds are spread across the prepared soil surface.
  • Row planting: Seeds are planted in organised rows.
  • Hill planting: Seeds are placed in selected planting stations.

7.2 Importance of correct spacing

  • Reduces competition for sunlight, water and nutrients.
  • Improves air movement around plants.
  • Supports easier weeding and crop inspection.
  • Allows efficient irrigation and field operations.
  • May reduce favourable conditions for some diseases.

7.3 Planting depth

Planting depth depends on seed size, soil type, moisture and crop requirements. Seeds planted too deeply may struggle to emerge, while shallow seeds may dry out or be exposed to birds and other hazards.

Always follow the recommended planting depth and spacing for the selected crop variety. There is no single spacing suitable for all crops.

8. Crop Nutrition and Fertilizer Management

Plants require essential nutrients to develop roots, leaves, stems, flowers, fruits and seeds. Nutrient deficiencies can reduce growth and crop production.

8.1 Major plant nutrients

  • Nitrogen (N): Supports leafy and vegetative growth.
  • Phosphorus (P): Supports root development and energy transfer.
  • Potassium (K): Supports water regulation and general plant functions.

8.2 Secondary and micronutrients

Plants also require nutrients such as calcium, magnesium, sulphur, iron, zinc, boron, manganese and copper. Requirements vary according to the crop and soil.

8.3 Organic nutrient sources

  • Well-decomposed animal manure.
  • Compost made from suitable organic materials.
  • Green manure crops.
  • Appropriate crop residues.

8.4 Responsible fertilizer application

  1. Test the soil where possible.
  2. Identify the crop's nutrient requirements.
  3. Select an appropriate fertilizer.
  4. Follow recommended application rates.
  5. Apply at the correct crop growth stage.
  6. Prevent fertilizer from entering water sources.
  7. Record the product, quantity and application date.

9. Water Management and Irrigation

Water is essential for seed germination, nutrient movement, photosynthesis and crop development. Proper water management helps crops survive dry periods and supports reliable production.

9.1 Common irrigation methods

Surface irrigation

Water moves across the soil surface through basins, borders or furrows. Proper land preparation and water control are important.

Drip irrigation

Water is delivered slowly near plant roots through emitters or drip lines. Filtration and regular maintenance help prevent blockages.

Sprinkler irrigation

Water is sprayed through nozzles and distributed over crops in droplets. Wind and operating pressure can affect distribution.

Manual irrigation

Water is applied using watering cans, buckets or hosepipes. It is useful for small gardens and nursery operations.

9.2 Water conservation practices

  • Use suitable irrigation systems.
  • Repair leaking pipes and damaged fittings.
  • Apply mulch to reduce evaporation.
  • Harvest and store rainwater where feasible.
  • Monitor soil moisture before irrigation.
  • Maintain drainage and avoid waterlogging.
  • Schedule irrigation according to crop needs.
Efficient irrigation means applying the right amount of water at the right time and in the right place. Excessive irrigation wastes water and can damage soil and crops.

10. Weed Management

Weeds are unwanted plants growing where crops are cultivated. They compete with crops for water, sunlight, nutrients and growing space.

10.1 Problems caused by weeds

  • Reduced crop growth and production.
  • Competition for soil moisture and nutrients.
  • Interference with harvesting.
  • Some weeds host pests and plant diseases.
  • Increased production and labour costs.

10.2 Weed control methods

Manual control

Removing weeds by hand or using appropriate hand tools.

Mechanical control

Using equipment to cultivate, cut or remove weeds.

Cultural control

Using crop rotation, mulching, suitable crop spacing and competitive crop varieties.

Chemical control

Applying approved herbicides according to the crop, weed species and product label.

Herbicides must be used carefully. Follow label instructions, protective equipment requirements, re-entry periods and pre-harvest intervals. Avoid spraying in unsuitable wind conditions or near sensitive crops and water sources.

11. Agricultural Pests and Integrated Pest Management

Agricultural pests are organisms that damage crops or interfere with agricultural production. They may include insects, mites, rodents, birds and other harmful organisms.

11.1 Examples of crop pests

  • Aphids.
  • Whiteflies.
  • Cutworms.
  • Fall armyworms.
  • Fruit flies.
  • Red spider mites.
  • Rodents and birds.

11.2 Common signs of pest damage

  • Holes in leaves and stems.
  • Curled or distorted leaves.
  • Damaged fruits and seeds.
  • Chewed seedlings.
  • Sticky substances on plant surfaces.
  • Visible insects, eggs or larvae.

11.3 Integrated Pest Management (IPM)

Integrated Pest Management is an approach that combines different control methods to keep pest populations at acceptable levels while reducing unnecessary pesticide use.

  1. Monitoring: Inspect crops regularly.
  2. Identification: Identify the pest correctly.
  3. Prevention: Use healthy seeds and good field practices.
  4. Biological control: Protect useful natural enemies.
  5. Physical control: Remove or trap pests where suitable.
  6. Cultural control: Rotate crops and manage crop residues.
  7. Chemical control: Use approved pesticides only when justified.
Correct pest identification should come before treatment. Not every insect found on a farm is harmful; some insects are beneficial pollinators or natural enemies of pests.

12. Crop Health and Disease Management

Crop health refers to the overall condition of plants and their ability to grow, develop and produce normally. Healthy crops are better able to tolerate environmental stress and produce quality harvests.

12.1 Common causes of crop diseases

  • Fungi.
  • Bacteria.
  • Viruses.
  • Nematodes.
  • Environmental and nutrient disorders that resemble disease symptoms.

12.2 Common warning signs

  • Yellowing leaves.
  • Wilting plants.
  • Leaf spots and lesions.
  • Root decay.
  • Stunted growth.
  • Unusual fruit or stem damage.

12.3 Prevention and management

  • Use healthy seeds and seedlings.
  • Choose suitable resistant varieties where available.
  • Maintain proper plant spacing.
  • Manage irrigation and field drainage.
  • Remove severely infected plant material safely.
  • Clean tools and equipment.
  • Seek professional diagnosis when symptoms are unclear.

13. Climate-Smart Farming

Climate-smart agriculture involves farming approaches that improve productivity, strengthen resilience to climate change and, where possible, reduce greenhouse gas emissions.

13.1 Climate-related farming challenges

  • Unpredictable rainfall.
  • Prolonged drought.
  • High temperatures.
  • Flooding and soil erosion.
  • Changes in pest and disease patterns.
  • Reduced availability of water.

13.2 Climate-smart practices

  • Plant drought-tolerant crop varieties where suitable.
  • Use weather forecasts to support planting decisions.
  • Harvest and conserve rainwater.
  • Maintain soil organic matter.
  • Use appropriate irrigation systems.
  • Diversify crops and farm activities.
  • Protect trees and useful vegetation.
  • Adjust planting dates when seasonal information supports it.
Farmers should combine weather forecasts, local observations and agricultural advice rather than relying on a single forecast to make major production decisions.

14. Soil and Water Conservation

Soil and water conservation involves protecting land and water resources from degradation, erosion and waste. It helps maintain long-term agricultural productivity.

14.1 Soil conservation practices

  • Mulching.
  • Contour farming on suitable slopes.
  • Terracing where appropriate.
  • Cover cropping.
  • Crop rotation.
  • Windbreaks and shelterbelts.
  • Maintaining suitable vegetation cover.

14.2 Water conservation practices

  • Rainwater harvesting.
  • Water storage in suitable tanks and reservoirs.
  • Efficient irrigation.
  • Repairing leaks.
  • Reducing unnecessary evaporation.
  • Protecting rivers, wells and boreholes from contamination.

15. Digital Agriculture and Smart Farming

Digital agriculture uses information and communication technologies to collect, analyse and share agricultural information. It helps farmers make better decisions about production and farm management.

15.1 Important digital agriculture tools

Weather applications

Provide weather forecasts, rainfall information and temperature estimates that can support farm planning.

Soil moisture sensors

Measure soil moisture at selected depths and help farmers assess when irrigation may be necessary.

GPS and digital mapping

Help identify farm boundaries, map fields and record geographical locations.

Satellite imagery

Can help monitor vegetation conditions, crop development and changes in land cover.

Mobile agricultural platforms

Can provide farming information, extension advice, market updates and communication between farmers and agricultural service providers.

Digital farm records

Electronic records can help track planting dates, input use, irrigation, labour, expenses, yields and sales.

15.2 Benefits of digital agriculture

  • Improved access to agricultural information.
  • Better farm planning and record keeping.
  • Early identification of possible production problems.
  • More informed irrigation decisions.
  • Improved market communication.
  • Monitoring crop conditions over time.
Digital information should be checked against actual field conditions. Sensors require correct installation and calibration, while satellite and weather information may have limitations in local accuracy.

16. Harvesting and Crop Maturity

Harvesting is the process of collecting mature crops from the field. Harvesting at the correct time helps protect crop quality, reduce losses and improve market value.

16.1 Signs of crop maturity

  • Crop colour changes according to its maturity stage.
  • Fruits reach the required size and firmness.
  • Grains become mature and reach suitable moisture levels.
  • Leaves or stems show normal maturity changes.
  • Crop-specific maturity indicators are achieved.

16.2 Good harvesting practices

  1. Use clean and suitable harvesting equipment.
  2. Harvest at the recommended maturity stage.
  3. Avoid unnecessary bruising and physical damage.
  4. Separate damaged and diseased produce.
  5. Use clean containers.
  6. Move produce away from excessive heat where appropriate.
  7. Record the quantity harvested.

17. Storage and Post-Harvest Management

Post-harvest management covers the activities carried out after harvesting to preserve quality and reduce losses before produce reaches consumers or markets.

17.1 Causes of post-harvest losses

  • Poor handling and transport.
  • Insect and rodent damage.
  • Excess moisture.
  • Unhygienic storage facilities.
  • High temperatures and unsuitable humidity.
  • Delayed marketing.
  • Poor packaging.

17.2 Good storage practices

  • Clean and inspect storage facilities.
  • Dry grains to safe moisture levels for the intended storage period.
  • Use suitable bags and containers.
  • Keep produce off damp floors.
  • Control insects and rodents safely.
  • Inspect stored produce regularly.
  • Use older stock first where appropriate.
Produce intended for storage must be adequately dried and protected. Safe grain moisture levels depend on the crop, storage conditions and intended storage duration.

18. Agricultural Marketing

Agricultural marketing involves moving farm products from producers to consumers through activities such as sorting, grading, packaging, transportation, pricing and selling.

18.1 Understanding the market

  • Identify potential customers and buyers.
  • Understand demand and seasonal price changes.
  • Compare prices in different markets.
  • Identify quality and packaging requirements.
  • Estimate transport and handling costs.
  • Consider collective marketing through farmer groups.

18.2 Value addition

Value addition means improving a product's market value through processing, sorting, grading, packaging, preservation or other appropriate activities.

Examples include:

  • Cleaning and grading grains.
  • Drying suitable fruits and vegetables.
  • Packaging produce for different market segments.
  • Processing selected crops into food products.

Farm records are written or digital documents that describe agricultural activities, inputs, production, expenses and sales.

19.1 Important farm records

20. Farm Business and Financial Management

A farm is also an economic activity. Farmers need to understand production costs, revenue, profit, investment and financial risks.

20.1 Common farm expenses

  • Seeds and seedlings.
  • Fertilizers and soil amendments.
  • Water and irrigation expenses.
  • Labour costs.
  • Tools and machinery.
  • Transport and packaging.
  • Storage and marketing expenses.

20.2 Understanding farm profit

Farm profit is calculated by subtracting total production and business costs from total revenue.

Farm profit = Total revenue − Total costs

For example, if a farmer receives KSh 50,000 from produce sales and spends KSh 35,000 on production and marketing, the estimated profit is KSh 15,000.

20.3 Importance of budgeting

  • Helps control unnecessary spending.
  • Identifies expected capital requirements.
  • Supports investment decisions.
  • Helps compare production costs and income.
  • Improves financial planning.

21. Farm Safety and Environmental Protection

Agricultural work involves risks from machinery, chemicals, tools, electricity, animals and environmental conditions. Safety measures protect farmers, workers, consumers and the environment.

21.1 Chemical safety

  • Read and follow product labels.
  • Use the recommended protective equipment.
  • Store chemicals in secure, labelled containers.
  • Keep chemicals away from children and food.
  • Never reuse chemical containers for drinking water.
  • Dispose of empty containers according to approved guidance.

21.2 Machinery and equipment safety

  • Inspect equipment before operation.
  • Use machinery only after proper training.
  • Switch off equipment before maintenance.
  • Keep children away from operating machinery.
  • Use safe electrical connections for pumps and equipment.

21.3 Environmental protection

  • Protect rivers, wells and boreholes from pollution.
  • Avoid unnecessary chemical application.
  • Prevent soil erosion and land degradation.
  • Manage agricultural waste responsibly.
  • Use water efficiently.
  • Protect useful insects and natural vegetation.

22. Common Farming Mistakes to Avoid

  1. Planting without understanding soil and climate conditions.
  2. Using poor-quality or unsuitable seeds.
  3. Planting at incorrect depth or spacing.
  4. Applying fertilizer without considering crop requirements.
  5. Over-irrigating or allowing crops to suffer water stress.
  6. Ignoring early signs of pests and diseases.
  7. Using pesticides without identifying the problem.
  8. Failing to keep farm records.
  9. Harvesting too early or too late.
  10. Storing produce in unsuitable conditions.
  11. Producing crops without understanding market demand.
  12. Ignoring weather forecasts and seasonal risks.

25. Conclusion

Successful farming depends on informed decisions, proper resource management and continuous learning. Farmers who understand soil, seeds, water, crop health, climate, harvesting and markets are better equipped to manage production challenges.

Digital agriculture provides additional opportunities through weather information, mobile learning, farm records, sensors, mapping and market communication. Combining these tools with practical field knowledge can support more productive and sustainable farming.

Farmers are encouraged to observe their fields, maintain accurate records, seek extension advice and adapt recommended practices to local conditions.
