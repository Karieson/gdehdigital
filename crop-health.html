Crop Health & Pest Management

Protect your crops through early observation, responsible pest management and informed agricultural practices. Learn how to recognise warning signs, reduce crop losses and make better decisions for healthier farms.

This page provides general educational information. Crop symptoms can have several causes, and the same symptom may indicate different problems. Seek advice from a county agricultural extension officer or qualified agronomist before applying treatment.

Healthy Crops, Stronger Harvests

Understand the health of your crops

Regular field observation helps farmers notice changes early. Identifying possible problems before they spread can help reduce unnecessary expenses, protect yields and support sustainable agricultural production.

Crop Monitoring

Common crop warning signs

Learn what to observe when plants begin to show unusual changes. These signs are starting points for investigation, not confirmed diagnoses.

01 — LEAF DAMAGE

Holes, Chewing and Damaged Leaves

Irregular holes, missing leaf edges or visible feeding marks may indicate insect activity or other sources of physical damage.

  • Inspect both upper and lower leaf surfaces.
  • Look for insects, eggs, larvae or droppings.
  • Check whether damage is spreading to new leaves.
02 — LEAF COLOUR

Yellowing or Discoloured Leaves

Changes in leaf colour may be associated with nutrient deficiencies, water stress, root damage, pests or disease.

  • Observe whether older or younger leaves are affected.
  • Check soil moisture and drainage conditions.
  • Look for patterns across different plants.
03 — WILTING

Drooping and Wilting Plants

Wilting can occur when plants cannot take up enough water. Root problems, pests, disease and extreme heat may also contribute.

  • Check soil moisture around affected plants.
  • Inspect stems and roots where appropriate.
  • Compare affected plants with healthy nearby plants.
04 — LEAF SPOTS

Spots, Patches and Unusual Marks

Dark, pale, dry or spreading marks on leaves may be linked to disease, environmental stress or physical injury.

  • Record the size, colour and location of the spots.
  • Check whether new spots are appearing.
  • Avoid handling or moving affected plant material unnecessarily.
05 — STEM AND ROOTS

Weak Stems and Root Damage

Broken, discoloured or damaged stems and roots can interfere with water and nutrient movement.

  • Look for unusual stem holes or soft areas.
  • Observe whether plants are easily falling over.
  • Check for root damage where safe and practical.
06 — FLOWERS AND FRUITS

Damaged Flowers and Fruits

Flower drop, fruit damage, holes or rotting may be associated with insects, disease, poor pollination or environmental stress.

  • Inspect flowers and young fruits regularly.
  • Look for feeding marks or unusual growth.
  • Record when the damage first appeared.

Practical Farm Management

How to inspect your farm

Field scouting means regularly checking crops for signs of pests, disease, water stress and other production challenges.

  • 1. Walk through the field. Observe different sections rather than inspecting only the edges.
  • 2. Examine several plants. Compare healthy-looking plants with those showing unusual signs.
  • 3. Inspect leaves carefully. Check both sides of leaves, stems, flowers and fruits.
  • 4. Check soil and water conditions. Observe moisture, drainage and signs of water stress.
  • 5. Record your observations. Note the date, crop, affected area and changes over time.
  • 6. Seek professional advice. Consult an agricultural extension officer when the cause is unclear.

What information should you collect?

If you suspect a crop problem, collect useful information before seeking advice.

  • Crop name and variety, if known.
  • Farm location and date symptoms were observed.
  • Clear photographs of affected plants.
  • Whether one plant, a small area or the whole field is affected.
  • Recent rainfall and irrigation activities.
  • Any fertilisers or crop protection products recently used.

Do not rely on a single photograph or symptom to confirm a pest or disease diagnosis.

Sustainable Crop Protection

Integrated Pest Management (IPM)

Integrated Pest Management combines different methods to prevent and manage crop pests while reducing unnecessary pesticide use and protecting people, animals and the environment.

Prevention

Use healthy planting materials, maintain field hygiene, practise appropriate crop rotation and manage weeds.

Monitoring

Inspect crops regularly and monitor pest populations and crop damage before deciding on intervention.

Non-chemical Control

Where appropriate, use physical, cultural and biological control methods recommended by agricultural experts.

Responsible Treatment

Use approved crop protection products only when necessary and in accordance with professional advice and label directions.

Responsible Crop Protection

Safe pesticide use

Incorrect pesticide use can harm farmers, consumers, livestock, beneficial insects and the environment. Always prioritise prevention and seek professional guidance.

Use approved products

Select only registered products approved for the specific crop and target pest or disease in Kenya.

Read the product label

Follow all instructions, including application directions, restrictions and safety precautions.

Wear protective equipment

Use the protective clothing and equipment specified on the product label.

Avoid unsafe mixing

Never mix products unless the label explicitly permits it and qualified guidance confirms compatibility.

Respect waiting periods

Observe the pre-harvest interval and restricted-entry period stated on the product label.

Protect water and livestock

Prevent contamination of water sources and keep people and animals away from treated areas as directed.

Store products securely

Keep products in their original labelled containers, locked away from children, food, animal feed and water.

Dispose of containers safely

Follow label instructions and local disposal requirements. Never reuse pesticide containers for household purposes.

Need help with a crop problem?

If your crops show unusual symptoms, seek advice from a qualified agricultural extension officer. Share clear photographs and your field observations to support a more informed assessment.
