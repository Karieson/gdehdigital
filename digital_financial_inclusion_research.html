Abstract

Digital financial services have become an important component of modern financial inclusion. Mobile money, digital payments and technology-enabled financial services have created opportunities for individuals and businesses to access, transfer and manage money with reduced dependence on traditional physical financial infrastructure.

Despite these developments, access to and effective use of digital financial services remains uneven. Factors including network connectivity, internet affordability, transaction costs, digital literacy, financial literacy, trust, privacy concerns, fraud and cybersecurity can influence the ability of individuals to benefit from digital finance.

This study examines digital financial inclusion and payment interoperability among youth in Garissa County, Northern Kenya. It investigates access, usage patterns, affordability, connectivity, literacy, trust, privacy, security, fraud, gender-related barriers, age-related differences, youth employment and entrepreneurship, payment interoperability and perceptions of Open Payments.

The study adopts a mixed-methods approach using a structured offline field survey containing quantitative, Likert-scale and qualitative questions. Respondents are assigned unique codes, while completed questionnaires are retained as field evidence. Quantitative responses will be analyzed using descriptive statistics and comparative analysis, while qualitative responses will be analyzed thematically.

The findings are expected to provide locally grounded evidence that can support policymakers, financial service providers, telecommunications companies, development organizations and community institutions seeking to strengthen inclusive, secure, affordable and interoperable digital payment ecosystems.

Keywords: digital financial inclusion; mobile money; digital payments; payment interoperability; Open Payments; youth; financial literacy; digital literacy; entrepreneurship; Garissa County; Northern Kenya.

Executive Summary

This research investigates the experiences of young people with digital financial services in Garissa County. The study is motivated by the growing importance of mobile money and digital payments in everyday economic activity and by the need to understand whether existing digital financial ecosystems provide meaningful and inclusive access.

The research goes beyond measuring whether individuals possess mobile-money accounts. It considers whether people can use digital financial services reliably, affordably and safely. It therefore examines infrastructure, costs, literacy, trust, security, privacy, fraud, gender, age and economic participation.

A particular focus is placed on payment interoperability and Open Payments. The research examines whether users perceive greater interoperability as an opportunity to reduce payment barriers and improve convenience, while also identifying concerns that should be addressed through appropriate safeguards.

The research will ultimately produce an evidence chain linking signed consent and coded field questionnaires to a structured dataset, statistical analysis, qualitative findings, conclusions and recommendations.

Chapter One — Introduction
Digital Financial Inclusion and Payment Interoperability Among Youth in Northern Kenya

1.1 Background of the Study

Digital technology has significantly changed the manner in which individuals, households and businesses access financial services. Mobile phones, internet connectivity and digital payment platforms increasingly provide alternative channels through which people can transfer funds, receive income, purchase goods and services, pay bills and participate in economic activities.

Digital financial inclusion extends the concept of traditional financial inclusion by recognizing that access to financial services increasingly depends on technological capabilities and digital infrastructure. Meaningful inclusion therefore involves more than simply possessing a financial account. Users need reliable access, affordable services, adequate digital skills, financial knowledge, trust and appropriate consumer protection.

Young people represent an important population within this transformation. Youth increasingly interact with digital financial services for personal transactions, employment, entrepreneurship, online commerce and social activities. Digital payments can reduce geographical barriers and create opportunities for young entrepreneurs to receive payments and access customers beyond their immediate physical surroundings.

Nevertheless, digital financial inclusion is affected by persistent barriers. These include the cost of internet access, network reliability, transaction charges, access to smartphones, limited digital literacy, financial literacy, fraud, privacy concerns and lack of confidence in digital platforms.

These issues become particularly important in areas where digital infrastructure and economic opportunities are unevenly distributed. Northern Kenya provides an important context for examining how young people experience the benefits and limitations of digital finance.

1.2 Digital Transformation and Financial Services

The digital transformation of financial services has reduced reliance on physical branches and cash-based transactions. Mobile-based services allow users to initiate financial transactions from locations where traditional financial institutions may be less accessible.

Digital platforms may also support small businesses by allowing entrepreneurs to receive customer payments, transfer funds to suppliers, pay workers and maintain financial records. For young people who operate informal or emerging businesses, these capabilities can be particularly valuable.

However, digital transformation may also reproduce existing inequalities when individuals lack the infrastructure, skills or financial resources required to participate effectively.

1.3 Financial Inclusion in Kenya

Kenya has developed a strong digital financial ecosystem, particularly through mobile money and mobile-based financial services. The country's experience demonstrates how technology can expand financial access and introduce new mechanisms for transferring and managing money.

Despite progress, financial inclusion should be considered in terms of actual usage and outcomes rather than account ownership alone. A person may have access to a digital financial account but still face practical barriers that prevent meaningful use.

This study therefore examines not only access but also frequency of use, affordability, confidence, security and the ability of digital financial services to support economic participation.

1.4 Digital Financial Inclusion

Digital financial inclusion refers to the availability and effective use of appropriate financial products and services through digital channels. It incorporates both financial access and the technological conditions necessary for individuals to participate.

For the purposes of this research, digital financial inclusion is viewed as a multidimensional concept involving access, affordability, reliability, usability, digital capability, financial capability, security and trust.

1.5 Youth and Digital Financial Services

Youth are increasingly exposed to digital technologies and may be important users of mobile money, digital payments, online marketplaces and technology-enabled employment opportunities.

At the same time, youth unemployment, limited access to capital and unstable income may make transaction costs and financial risks more significant. Digital financial services may create opportunities for entrepreneurship, but only where the wider digital ecosystem is accessible and affordable.

1.6 Northern Kenya Context

Northern Kenya contains communities with diverse socioeconomic circumstances and varying access to infrastructure and services. Geographic distance, connectivity challenges and differences in economic opportunities can influence access to digital technologies.

Understanding digital financial inclusion in this context requires primary evidence from users themselves. National statistics can provide important background information, but local field research can reveal practical barriers and experiences that may not be visible in national aggregates.

1.7 Garissa County Context

Garissa County provides the geographical focus of this study. The research examines how young people within the county interact with digital financial services and how they perceive the emerging possibilities associated with payment interoperability.

The study recognizes that experiences may vary within the county. Respondents may differ in age, gender, education, employment status, occupation, location, digital skills and access to network infrastructure. These differences will be captured through the survey instrument and incorporated into the analysis.

1.8 Payment Interoperability

Payment interoperability refers broadly to the ability of different payment systems, providers or platforms to interact in ways that allow users to conduct transactions across otherwise separate systems.

Greater interoperability may reduce friction by giving users greater flexibility in selecting payment channels. It may also support businesses that serve customers using different financial-service providers.

However, interoperability must be accompanied by effective security, privacy, consumer protection and dispute-resolution mechanisms. Interoperability without adequate safeguards could potentially increase exposure to fraud or unauthorized access.

1.9 Open Payments

Within this research, Open Payments refers to payment arrangements that seek to facilitate greater connectivity and interoperability among payment systems while maintaining appropriate regulatory, security and consumer-protection safeguards.

The study does not assume that Open Payments automatically produce financial inclusion. Instead, it investigates whether users perceive potential benefits, what risks they identify and what conditions they believe would be necessary for successful implementation.

1.10 Statement of the Problem

Digital financial services have created substantial opportunities for financial participation, yet access to and effective use of these services remains affected by technological, economic and social barriers.

Young people may experience difficulties related to network coverage, internet costs, transaction charges, digital literacy, financial literacy, fraud, privacy and trust. These challenges can reduce the practical value of digital financial services even when the services are technically available.

Furthermore, there is a need for stronger local evidence regarding how young people in Garissa County experience digital financial services and how they perceive payment interoperability and Open Payments.

Without such evidence, interventions designed to promote digital financial inclusion may overlook the practical realities faced by users. This study therefore seeks to generate primary field evidence that can contribute to a more locally responsive understanding of digital financial inclusion.

1.11 Justification of the Study

The study is justified by the increasing importance of digital payments in everyday economic activity and the need to understand whether technological progress is translating into meaningful financial inclusion.

The research also responds to the need for evidence from Northern Kenya, where local experiences may differ from national averages. By collecting primary data directly from respondents, the study can identify practical barriers and opportunities that may otherwise remain underrepresented.

1.12 Significance of the Study

The study may benefit several groups.

  • Policymakers: by providing evidence that can inform inclusive digital-finance policies.
  • Financial service providers: by identifying user concerns regarding costs, access, trust and interoperability.
  • Telecommunications and technology providers: by highlighting connectivity and usability challenges.
  • Youth organizations: by identifying opportunities for digital and financial literacy.
  • Development organizations: by providing evidence for community-level financial inclusion interventions.
  • Researchers: by contributing local evidence to the broader literature on digital financial inclusion.

1.16 Scope of the Study

The study focuses on digital financial inclusion and payment interoperability among youth in Garissa County. The research covers access, usage, affordability, connectivity, literacy, trust, privacy, security, fraud, gender, age, entrepreneurship, interoperability and perceptions of Open Payments.

The study primarily examines user experiences rather than technical engineering aspects of payment-system architecture. Technical and regulatory issues are considered to the extent that they influence user-level financial inclusion.

1.17 Limitations and Delimitations

The study may face limitations associated with sample size, respondent availability, geographical coverage, recall bias and the possibility that respondents may be reluctant to disclose negative financial experiences.

These limitations will be addressed through clear questionnaire instructions, voluntary participation, respondent coding, careful data management and transparent reporting of methodological limitations.

Chapter Two — Literature Review
Digital Financial Inclusion, Youth and Payment Interoperability

2.1 Introduction

This chapter reviews the conceptual and empirical literature relevant to digital financial inclusion, digital payments, mobile money, payment interoperability and Open Payments. It considers evidence from global, African and Kenyan contexts and identifies areas requiring further local investigation.

2.2 Global Perspective

Globally, digital financial services have expanded the range of channels through which individuals can access financial products. Mobile phones, digital platforms and electronic payment systems can reduce physical barriers and allow users to participate in financial transactions without relying entirely on traditional banking infrastructure.

However, global research also demonstrates that digital access can be uneven. Individuals with lower incomes, limited digital skills or poor connectivity may face greater barriers to effective use.

2.3 African Perspective

African countries have experienced significant growth in mobile-money services and technology-enabled financial systems. Mobile money has demonstrated the potential of digital channels to reach populations that may be underserved by conventional banking systems.

Nevertheless, challenges relating to affordability, network reliability, digital skills, fraud and consumer protection remain important.

2.4 Kenyan Perspective

Kenya provides an important case for studying digital financial inclusion because mobile money has become deeply integrated into everyday economic transactions. Digital payments are used for personal transfers, business transactions, bill payments and other activities.

The Kenyan experience also illustrates the importance of interoperability, competition, consumer protection and infrastructure in determining how users experience digital financial services.

2.5 Digital Financial Inclusion Among Youth

Youth may have comparatively high exposure to digital technology, but technological familiarity does not necessarily translate into financial inclusion. Young people may still face income constraints, transaction costs, limited financial knowledge and employment instability.

2.6 Mobile Money Adoption

Mobile money can provide convenient mechanisms for sending and receiving funds. Its accessibility through mobile devices can reduce the need for users to travel to physical financial institutions.

The continued importance of mobile money makes it necessary to examine both its positive effects and the practical difficulties users encounter.

2.7 Digital Payment Usage

Digital payment usage encompasses transactions involving mobile money, electronic transfers, online payment platforms and other digital mechanisms. Usage patterns can reveal whether digital financial services are primarily used for personal transfers or whether they have become integrated into economic activities and businesses.

2.8 Transaction Costs and Affordability

Transaction costs can influence how frequently users make digital payments. For individuals with low or irregular incomes, even relatively small charges may affect decisions regarding payment methods.

Affordability also extends beyond transaction charges to include the cost of mobile devices, internet access, data bundles and travel required to access agents.

2.9 Internet and Network Accessibility

Reliable connectivity is a fundamental requirement for many digital financial services. Network interruptions can delay transactions, create uncertainty and potentially result in failed or duplicated payment attempts.

2.10 Digital and Financial Literacy

Digital literacy involves the ability to use technology effectively and safely, while financial literacy involves knowledge and skills related to financial decision-making.

The combination of both forms of literacy is important for digital financial inclusion because users must be able to operate digital platforms while understanding financial costs and risks.

2.11 Trust and Consumer Confidence

Trust can influence willingness to use digital financial services. Concerns about failed transactions, fraud, privacy and poor customer support may reduce confidence.

2.12 Privacy and Data Protection

Digital financial transactions generate information about users and their financial activity. Users may therefore have concerns regarding how personal and transactional information is collected, stored, processed and shared.

2.13 Fraud and Cybersecurity

Fraud represents one of the most important risks associated with digital financial services. Users may encounter phishing attempts, social engineering, unauthorized transactions, fraudulent requests or other forms of digital exploitation.

Digital financial inclusion therefore requires not only access but also effective security awareness and consumer protection.

2.14 Gender and Digital Financial Inclusion

Gender may influence access to digital devices, financial resources, digital skills and decision-making power. The study therefore examines whether female and male respondents report different experiences.

2.15 Age and Digital Financial Inclusion

Different age groups may demonstrate different levels of digital familiarity, confidence and financial independence. Age-related comparisons can therefore help identify groups that may require targeted interventions.

2.16 Youth Employment and Entrepreneurship

Digital payments may support youth entrepreneurship by enabling businesses to receive payments remotely and participate in digital marketplaces.

The study investigates whether respondents perceive digital financial services as contributing to employment, self-employment, online work and business growth.

2.17 Payment Interoperability

Interoperability can potentially reduce fragmentation by allowing users to transact across different payment systems. It may improve convenience for both individuals and businesses.

However, interoperability also requires effective standards, security, dispute-resolution mechanisms and clear consumer information.

2.18 Open Payments

Open Payments represent an opportunity to explore how greater payment connectivity can support financial inclusion. The literature suggests that openness and interoperability should be considered alongside privacy, security, consumer protection and regulatory oversight.

2.19 Research Gap

Although substantial literature exists on digital financial inclusion and mobile money, there remains a need for more locally grounded evidence regarding how youth in Northern Kenya experience digital financial services.

In particular, the intersection between digital financial inclusion, youth economic participation, payment interoperability and perceptions of Open Payments requires further investigation at community level.

This study addresses that gap by collecting primary evidence directly from respondents in Garissa County.

Chapter Three — Theoretical and Conceptual Framework
Analytical Foundation of the Study

3.1 Introduction

This chapter presents the theoretical and conceptual foundations guiding the study. The framework links digital access, affordability, digital and financial literacy, trust, security and interoperability with outcomes associated with financial inclusion and economic participation.

3.2 Technology Acceptance Perspective

Technology acceptance perspectives emphasize factors influencing whether individuals are willing and able to use technological systems. Perceived usefulness, ease of use, confidence and trust may influence adoption and continued use.

Within this study, these concepts are relevant because availability of digital financial services does not necessarily guarantee adoption. Users must perceive services as useful, understandable, accessible and sufficiently safe.

3.3 Financial Inclusion Perspective

Financial inclusion emphasizes access to appropriate financial services and the ability to use them effectively. Digital financial inclusion extends this perspective by incorporating technological access, affordability and digital capability.

3.4 Digital Divide Perspective

The digital divide perspective recognizes inequalities in access to technology, connectivity, skills and meaningful digital participation.

This perspective is particularly important for the study because differences in internet access, device ownership and digital literacy may affect financial inclusion outcomes.

3.5 Conceptual Framework

The conceptual framework proposes that digital financial inclusion is influenced by a combination of access factors, user-level factors and payment-system characteristics. These factors interact to influence financial participation and economic outcomes.

Chapter Four — Research Methodology
Research Design, Fieldwork, Data Management and Analysis

4.1 Introduction

This chapter describes the methodology that will be used to collect, manage and analyze primary evidence for the study. The methodology is designed to ensure that the final findings are traceable to the completed field questionnaires.

4.2 Research Design

The study adopts a mixed-methods descriptive research design. The quantitative component provides measurable evidence regarding access, usage, affordability, literacy, trust and other indicators, while the qualitative component captures respondents' personal experiences, perceptions and recommendations.

Combining the two approaches allows the study to identify measurable patterns while preserving the experiences behind those patterns.

4.3 Study Area

The study will be conducted in Garissa County, Northern Kenya. Field activities will seek to capture respondents from relevant communities and locations in order to provide variation in digital access and socioeconomic circumstances.

4.4 Target Population

The primary target population consists of youth and relevant users of digital financial services in Garissa County. Respondents will be selected to capture diversity in age, gender, education, employment, occupation and location.

4.5 Sampling Strategy

The study will use a structured field sampling strategy appropriate to the available population and research resources. Where practical, respondents will be distributed across relevant demographic and geographic categories to reduce overrepresentation of a single group.

4.6 Sample Size

The final sample size will be established before field analysis and documented transparently in the final research report.

Data Integrity Note: The final sample size must reflect the actual number of valid completed questionnaires. It should not be increased or estimated after data collection simply to produce a stronger-looking result.

4.7 Inclusion Criteria

  • Respondents who fall within the defined youth study population.
  • Respondents located within the study area.
  • Respondents able and willing to provide informed consent.
  • Respondents who voluntarily complete the questionnaire.

4.8 Exclusion Criteria

  • Individuals outside the defined target population.
  • Individuals who do not provide consent.
  • Questionnaires containing insufficient information for meaningful analysis.

4.9 Survey Instrument

Data will be collected using a professionally structured offline paper questionnaire. The questionnaire has been designed to be completed manually and includes a respondent consent statement.

The instrument contains both closed-ended and open-ended questions. Closed-ended questions generate quantitative data, while open-ended questions provide qualitative information concerning personal experiences, challenges and recommendations.

4.11 Pilot Testing

Before full field deployment, the questionnaire should be pilot-tested with a small number of respondents who are representative of the target population but are not necessarily included in the final analytical sample.

Pilot testing will help identify unclear wording, repetitive questions, inappropriate response categories, excessive questionnaire length and other practical problems.

4.12 Validity

Content validity will be strengthened by ensuring that the questionnaire directly corresponds to the research objectives and research questions. Each major research objective should be represented by one or more survey indicators.

4.13 Reliability

Reliability will be supported through consistent questionnaire wording, standardized administration procedures, clear coding rules and careful data-entry verification.

Where appropriate, internal consistency of groups of Likert-scale items may be assessed during quantitative analysis.

4.14 Field Data Collection Procedure

Step 1 — Preparation: Print and organize the questionnaires and consent information.
Step 2 — Respondent Introduction: Explain the purpose of the research and voluntary nature of participation.
Step 3 — Consent: Obtain informed consent before questionnaire completion.
Step 4 — Coding: Assign a unique code such as GAR-YOUTH-001.
Step 5 — Completion: Respondent completes the questionnaire independently where practical.
Step 6 — Review: Check the questionnaire for completeness without changing responses.
Step 7 — Secure Storage: Store completed paper questionnaires securely.
Step 8 — Data Entry: Transfer coded responses into the research dataset.
Step 9 — Verification: Compare entered data against the original questionnaires.

Completed questionnaires will be retained as original field evidence. The analytical dataset will contain respondent codes and survey responses rather than unnecessary personal identifiers.

Data entry should be conducted carefully and verified against original questionnaires. Missing responses should be coded consistently and should not be converted into arbitrary answers.

  • Use standardized questionnaires.
  • Train data collectors where applicable.
  • Check questionnaires for completeness.
  • Maintain consistent respondent codes.
  • Verify entered data against original questionnaires.
  • Document missing data.
  • Avoid altering respondent answers.
  • Preserve original completed questionnaires securely.

4.20 Ethical Considerations

Participation will be voluntary. Respondents will be informed about the purpose of the study and will have the opportunity to decline participation or skip questions they do not wish to answer.

The study will avoid collecting unnecessary personally identifiable information. Names, national identification numbers, passwords, PINs and similar authentication information should not be recorded in the research dataset.

Completed questionnaires will be stored securely and used only for the stated research purposes. Findings will be reported in aggregated or anonymized form.

4.22 Research Integrity

Research Integrity Principle

The final results chapters will be populated only after actual questionnaires have been collected and analyzed. Frequencies, percentages, statistical comparisons, respondent quotations and conclusions will be derived from the collected evidence rather than estimated or fabricated.
