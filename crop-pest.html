Pests and Crops

A practical learning resource for farmers on identifying crop pests, recognising damage, using digital monitoring tools and applying appropriate pest management practices.

After studying this module, a farmer should be able to:

  • Explain what crop pests are and how they affect farming.

  • Keep simple records of pest observations and farm actions.

01. Understanding Crop Pests

Crop pests are organisms that damage cultivated plants by feeding on their leaves, stems, roots, flowers, seeds or fruits. Some pests also spread plant diseases.

Pest attacks can reduce crop growth, lower harvest quantities, affect produce quality and increase the cost of farming.

Pest problems can occur at different stages of crop production, from seed planting and seedling establishment to flowering, fruit development and storage.

Why Farmers Need to Understand Crop Pests

  • To recognise pest attacks before damage becomes severe.
  • To protect crops and reduce avoidable losses.
  • To choose appropriate and safe control methods.
  • To protect beneficial insects and the farm environment.
  • To improve farm planning and productivity.
Farmer's principle: Regular field observation is one of the simplest ways to detect crop problems early. Do not wait until an entire field shows visible damage.

02. Common Types of Crop Pests

A. Insect Pests

Insect pests feed on plant tissues, suck plant sap, bore into stems or damage developing fruits.

Pest Common Damage Commonly Affected Crops
Fall armyworm Ragged holes, leaf scraping and damage to the growing point. Maize and other suitable host crops.
Aphids Suck sap, causing curling, yellowing and stunted growth. Vegetables, legumes and other crops.
Whiteflies Suck sap and may transmit plant viruses. Tomatoes, beans and other vegetables.
Stem borers Tunnel inside stems, weakening plants and damaging growing points. Maize, sorghum and other cereals.
Locusts Consume leaves and other green plant materials. Many field crops and pasture plants.
Thrips Cause silvery scars, leaf distortion and flower damage. Onions, vegetables and other crops.
Fruit flies Larvae feed inside fruits, causing decay and premature fruit drop. Mangoes and other susceptible fruits.

B. Rodents

Rats, mice and other field rodents can feed on planted seeds, young plants, mature crops and stored produce.

C. Birds

Some birds feed on planted seeds and ripening grain. They can cause significant losses in cereal crops, including sorghum, millet and maize.

D. Plant-Parasitic Nematodes

These microscopic roundworms attack plant roots. Some cause root knots, root lesions and poor water and nutrient absorption.

E. Other Crop-Damaging Organisms

Slugs, snails and certain mites can also damage crops. Farmers should distinguish pest damage from disease, nutrient deficiency and environmental stress.

Important distinction: Not every insect found on a farm is harmful. Bees, ladybird beetles, spiders and other beneficial organisms can support crop production by pollinating plants or controlling pests naturally.

03. Recognising Signs of Pest Damage

Farmers should inspect leaves, stems, roots, flowers and fruits for unusual changes.

Common Signs to Look For

  • Holes, cuts or irregular feeding marks on leaves.
  • Yellowing, curling or distorted leaves.
  • Damaged stems or small holes in stalks.
  • Wilting or weak plants.
  • Missing or damaged seedlings.
  • Root damage or unusual root growth.
  • Damaged, discoloured or rotting fruits.
  • Insects, eggs, larvae or droppings on plants.
  • Sticky substances or unusual insect activity.
  • Reduced crop growth or poor development.
Observation tip: Record the affected crop, the part of the plant damaged, the location, the date and the extent of the damage. Take clear photographs where possible.

Do Not Confuse Pest Damage With Other Problems

Yellow leaves, wilting and poor growth may also result from drought, waterlogging, nutrient deficiencies, diseases or herbicide injury. Examine the plant carefully before deciding on a treatment.

04. Crop Pest Identification

Correct identification helps farmers select an effective management method. Identification should be based on the organism observed, the crop affected and the type of damage.

Basic Identification Procedure

  1. Identify the crop and its growth stage.
  2. Inspect the affected leaves, stems, roots or fruits.
  3. Look for insects, larvae, eggs or other visible evidence.
  4. Photograph the pest and the damage clearly.
  5. Record the date and location of the observation.
  6. Compare the evidence with reliable agricultural information.
  7. Ask an agricultural extension officer to verify uncertain cases.

How to Take Useful Pest Photographs

  • Use good natural lighting.
  • Keep the camera focused on the pest.
  • Take a close-up photograph and another showing the whole plant.
  • Include a clear view of the damaged plant part.
  • Avoid disturbing the insect before taking the photograph.
  • Save the photograph with the crop name and observation date.
Remember: A photograph may support preliminary identification, but it does not always provide enough evidence for a definite diagnosis.

05. Digital Technologies for Pest Monitoring

Agro-digital technology helps farmers collect, store, share and analyse information about crop pests. These tools can improve communication and support better farm management decisions.

A. Smartphone Photography

Farmers can use smartphone cameras to capture images of suspicious insects, damaged leaves and affected crops. Images can be shared with agricultural experts for advice.

B. Artificial Intelligence (AI)

AI-powered applications can analyse crop photographs and suggest possible pest or plant-health problems. Their results should be treated as preliminary guidance.

C. GPS and Digital Mapping

GPS-enabled devices can record the location of a pest observation. Location information helps farmers and agricultural officers identify areas requiring inspection.

D. Digital Reporting Systems

Mobile applications, SMS services and online platforms can help farmers report outbreaks and communicate with agricultural extension services.

E. Drones and Satellite Technology

Drones and satellite imagery can help identify unusual crop conditions and areas that need closer inspection. Field checks are still needed to confirm the cause of crop damage.

Digital Tools Farmers Can Explore

Plantix

A crop advisory application that uses photographs to help assess possible crop health problems.

FAO FAMEWS

A fall armyworm monitoring and early warning system supporting field monitoring and reporting.

iNaturalist

A biodiversity observation platform that helps users document and explore living organisms.

Google Lens

A visual search tool that can provide preliminary information about photographed organisms.

Digital safety: Applications may produce incorrect results. Do not purchase or apply pesticides solely because an app has suggested a particular pest or treatment. Verify the problem with a qualified agricultural extension officer.

06. Integrated Pest Management (IPM)

Integrated Pest Management is a practical approach that combines different pest control methods to keep pest populations at manageable levels while reducing unnecessary risks to people, animals and the environment.

Main IPM Methods

Method How It Works Example
Cultural control Uses farming practices that reduce pest problems. Crop rotation, field sanitation and timely planting.
Biological control Uses natural enemies to reduce pest populations. Conserving ladybird beetles and other beneficial organisms.
Mechanical control Physically removes or destroys pests. Hand-picking caterpillars where practical.
Physical control Uses barriers and traps to prevent or reduce pest activity. Protective nets and suitable traps.
Chemical control Uses approved pesticides when justified. Applying a registered product according to its label.
Digital monitoring Supports observation, reporting and decision-making. Mobile pest records and GPS mapping.

The IPM Decision Process

  1. Inspect the crop regularly.
  2. Identify the pest or suspected cause of damage.
  3. Monitor the level and spread of infestation.
  4. Consider the crop stage and potential economic damage.
  5. Choose suitable preventive or control methods.
  6. Use pesticides only when necessary and as directed.
  7. Inspect the crop again and record the results.
IPM principle: The aim is not necessarily to eliminate every insect. The aim is to prevent unacceptable crop damage while protecting beneficial organisms and the environment.

07. Preventing and Managing Crop Pests

Good Farm Practices

  • Use clean, healthy and suitable planting materials.
  • Inspect crops regularly, including the underside of leaves.
  • Remove heavily infested plant material where appropriate.
  • Control weeds that may host crop pests.
  • Practise crop rotation where suitable.
  • Maintain good field sanitation.
  • Use recommended planting dates and spacing.
  • Protect stored produce from rodents and insects.
  • Conserve beneficial insects and natural enemies.
  • Seek professional guidance when pest damage increases.

Safe Pesticide Use

When chemical control is necessary, farmers should:

  • Use only products registered and approved for the intended crop and pest.
  • Read and follow the product label instructions.
  • Wear the protective equipment specified on the label.
  • Observe the pre-harvest interval before harvesting.
  • Follow the re-entry interval before entering treated fields.
  • Never mix products unless the label or a qualified expert permits it.
  • Keep pesticides away from children, animals, food and water sources.
  • Dispose of containers safely according to local guidance.
Safety warning: Never use an unlabelled pesticide, an unknown chemical mixture or a product intended for another purpose. Follow Kenyan pesticide registration and safety guidance.

08. Practical Farmer Field Inspection

Field inspection helps farmers detect crop problems before they spread. A farmer can use the following procedure during routine farm visits.

Step-by-Step Inspection

  1. Walk through different parts of the farm.
  2. Inspect a number of plants rather than relying on one plant.
  3. Check leaves, stems, growing points, flowers and fruits.
  4. Look for insects, eggs, larvae and signs of feeding.
  5. Observe whether damage is isolated or widespread.
  6. Photograph suspicious pests and crop symptoms.
  7. Record the observations and seek advice where necessary.
  8. Return to inspect the affected area after taking action.

Farmer's Field Inspection Checklist

  • Crop type and variety recorded.
  • Crop growth stage identified.
  • Leaves inspected for damage.
  • Stems and growing points checked.
  • Roots inspected where necessary.
  • Insects or other suspected pests observed.
  • Photographs captured.
  • Date and farm location recorded.
  • Action or advice documented.
  • Follow-up inspection planned.
Practical activity: Visit a crop field, select several plants from different areas and record any pest evidence. Compare healthy plants with affected plants.

Information Worth Recording

  • Farm location or GPS coordinates, where available.
