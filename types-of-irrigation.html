01. Introduction to Irrigation

Irrigation is the artificial application of water to agricultural land to support crop growth when rainfall is insufficient or does not occur at the right time.

Crops require water for seed germination, nutrient absorption, photosynthesis, plant growth, flowering, fruit development and other biological processes.

In areas where rainfall is unreliable, irrigation helps farmers maintain crop production during dry periods. It is particularly important in arid and semi-arid regions, including parts of northern and eastern Kenya.

How Irrigation Works

An irrigation system collects water from a source, transports it through a suitable structure or network, and applies it to the crop root zone or growing area.

Depending on the method, water may flow over the soil, be sprayed into the air, drip from emitters or move through pipes installed beneath the soil.

Key concept: The purpose of irrigation is to provide crops with sufficient water at the appropriate time and in the appropriate quantity, while avoiding water wastage and damage to the soil.

02. Importance of Irrigation in Agriculture

A. Supports Crop Production

Irrigation supplies water when rainfall is inadequate, helping crops continue growing during dry periods.

B. Reduces Dependence on Rainfall

Farmers can plan crop production more reliably when they have access to a dependable water source.

C. Supports Dry-Season Farming

Irrigation makes it possible to grow suitable crops during periods when rain-fed production is difficult.

D. Improves Crop Quality

Appropriate water management supports healthy plant development and can improve the size, appearance and quality of produce.

E. Supports Crop Diversification

Access to irrigation can allow farmers to grow vegetables, fruits and other crops that require regular water supplies.

F. Supports Food Security and Income

Well-managed irrigation can increase production opportunities, improve farm income and support household food supplies.

G. Enables More Predictable Farm Planning

Farmers can plan planting, harvesting, labour and marketing activities more effectively when water availability is properly managed.

Important: Irrigation does not automatically guarantee higher yields. Crop performance also depends on suitable seeds, soil fertility, pest management, crop care, water quality and good agricultural practices.

03. Sources of Irrigation Water

Farmers obtain irrigation water from different sources. The suitability of each source depends on availability, water quality, distance, cost, reliability and legal requirements.

A. Rivers and Streams

Rivers and streams can provide water for irrigation. Farmers may use pumps, canals or gravity-fed systems to transport water from these sources.

Considerations:

  • Water availability may change with the seasons.
  • Water abstraction may require permits or approvals.
  • Flooding and sediment can affect water infrastructure.
  • Water must be assessed for quality and suitability.

B. Boreholes and Wells

Boreholes and wells provide access to groundwater. Water is commonly lifted using a pump and delivered to the field through pipes or storage tanks.

Considerations:

  • Drilling and pumping can be expensive.
  • Groundwater levels may decline if extraction is excessive.
  • Water salinity and other quality problems may occur.
  • Water abstraction should follow applicable regulations.

C. Dams and Water Pans

Dams and water pans collect and store water, especially during rainy periods. Stored water can be used during dry seasons where storage and water quality are adequate.

D. Harvested Rainwater

Rainwater can be collected from roofs, paved surfaces and specially prepared catchment areas. It can be directed into tanks, ponds or storage reservoirs.

E. Irrigation Canals

Canals transport water from rivers, reservoirs or other sources to agricultural areas. Some operate through gravity, while others are supported by pumps.

F. Treated Wastewater

Properly treated wastewater can be used for certain agricultural purposes when it meets relevant health and environmental standards.

Water safety: Untreated sewage and contaminated wastewater should not be used for crop irrigation. Water reuse must comply with public-health and environmental standards.

04. Classification of Irrigation Methods

Irrigation methods are commonly classified according to how water is delivered and distributed to crops.

01. Surface Irrigation

Water flows across the soil surface using gravity.

02. Sprinkler Irrigation

Water is sprayed into the air and falls onto crops like rainfall.

03. Drip Irrigation

Water is delivered slowly near plant roots through emitters.

04. Subsurface Irrigation

Water is supplied below the soil surface to the root zone.

Manual watering is also commonly used on small farms and gardens. It involves applying water using containers or handheld equipment.

05. Surface Irrigation

Surface irrigation is a method in which water moves over the surface of agricultural land through gravity. It is one of the oldest and most widely used irrigation approaches.

How Surface Irrigation Works

  1. Water is collected from a river, canal, reservoir or other source.
  2. It is directed into a field through a main channel or inlet.
  3. Water flows over the soil surface under gravity.
  4. It infiltrates into the soil and becomes available to plant roots.
  5. Excess water may drain away through suitable drainage channels.

Types of Surface Irrigation

A. Basin Irrigation

Basin irrigation divides the land into small, level enclosed areas surrounded by low soil bunds. Water enters each basin and spreads across the surface.

Suitable applications:

  • Rice production in appropriately prepared fields.
  • Some fruit trees grown in individual basins.
  • Other crops on suitably levelled land.

B. Furrow Irrigation

Furrow irrigation uses small channels constructed between crop rows. Water flows along the furrows and infiltrates into the soil towards plant roots.

Common applications:

  • Maize.
  • Sugarcane.
  • Cotton.
  • Some vegetable crops.
  • Other row-planted crops.

C. Border Irrigation

Border irrigation divides a field into long strips separated by low ridges. Water flows down each strip from the upper end towards the lower end.

It is commonly used on suitable, gently sloping land where water can be distributed evenly.

Advantages of Surface Irrigation

  • Can have relatively low equipment costs.
  • May use gravity instead of continuous pumping.
  • Can be suitable for large fields.
  • Does not require complex equipment in basic systems.
  • Can be operated and maintained using local labour.

Disadvantages of Surface Irrigation

  • May require land levelling and field preparation.
  • Water distribution can be uneven.
  • Excess water may cause waterlogging.
  • Water can be lost through runoff and deep percolation.
  • May increase soil erosion on unsuitable slopes.
  • Requires careful water-flow management.
Farmer's tip: Furrows should be designed according to soil type, field slope and water flow. Excessively long or steep furrows may result in uneven water application and erosion.

06. Sprinkler Irrigation

Sprinkler irrigation applies water through pressurised pipes and nozzles. Water is sprayed into the air and falls onto the soil and crops in a manner similar to rainfall.

How Sprinkler Irrigation Works

  1. Water is obtained from a suitable source.
  2. A pump creates the required pressure where necessary.
  3. Water passes through pipes or hoses.
  4. Sprinkler heads release water through nozzles.
  5. Water droplets are distributed over the field.
  6. Water infiltrates into the soil and reaches crop roots.

Types of Sprinkler Irrigation

A. Fixed Sprinkler System

Sprinkler heads are installed at fixed locations. They cover designated areas when the system operates.

B. Portable Sprinkler System

Pipes and sprinklers can be moved from one section of the farm to another.

C. Semi-Portable System

Some parts of the system remain fixed while other components, such as lateral pipes and sprinklers, are moved between irrigation locations.

D. Centre Pivot Irrigation

A long sprinkler line rotates around a central pivot point, usually watering a large circular area. It is commonly used on large agricultural farms.

E. Travelling Sprinkler System

A sprinkler unit moves across a field while applying water. It is used in suitable field layouts and agricultural operations.

Advantages of Sprinkler Irrigation

  • Can distribute water over a large area.
  • Suitable for many crops and soil types.
  • Can reduce the need for extensive land levelling.
  • Can be automated using timers and control systems.
  • Can be adapted to different field layouts.

Disadvantages of Sprinkler Irrigation

  • Requires pressure and energy to operate.
  • Wind can affect water distribution.
  • Some water may be lost through evaporation.
  • Nozzles can become blocked.
  • Equipment may require higher initial investment.
  • Wet foliage may increase certain disease risks in some crops.
Practical advice: Avoid operating sprinklers during strong winds or excessive heat when possible. Choose suitable operating times and inspect the distribution pattern regularly.

07. Drip Irrigation

Drip irrigation is a method that delivers water slowly and directly to the soil near plant roots through a network of pipes, tubes and small outlets called emitters.

It is especially useful where water is limited because it can apply water to the root zone while reducing unnecessary wetting of the spaces between plants.

How Drip Irrigation Works

  1. Water is collected from a suitable source or storage tank.
  2. A pump or gravity system supplies water to the network.
  3. A filter removes particles that could block emitters.
  4. Water moves through main and sub-main pipes.
  5. Drip lines carry water along crop rows.
  6. Emitters release controlled amounts of water near plant roots.

Main Components of a Drip System

Component Function
Water source Provides irrigation water.
Pump Provides pressure where required.
Filter Removes particles that may block emitters.
Fertiliser injector Introduces suitable soluble fertiliser into irrigation water when properly managed.
Main pipe Transports water from the source to the distribution network.
Sub-main pipe Distributes water to different irrigation sections.
Drip lines Carry water along crop rows.
Emitters Release water slowly near plant roots.
Valves Control water flow and irrigation sections.
Pressure regulator Maintains suitable operating pressure.

Types of Drip Irrigation

A. Surface Drip Irrigation

Drip lines are placed on the soil surface near crop rows. Emitters release water onto the soil.

B. Subsurface Drip Irrigation

Drip lines are installed below the soil surface. Water is released underground near the crop root zone.

Advantages of Drip Irrigation

  • Can use water efficiently when properly designed and maintained.
  • Delivers water close to plant roots.
  • Can reduce evaporation from wet soil surfaces.
  • Can reduce weed growth between crop rows.
  • Can be used for vegetables, fruits and other suitable crops.
  • Supports fertigation when appropriate equipment and practices are used.
  • Can be automated using timers and sensors.

Disadvantages of Drip Irrigation

  • Initial installation costs can be high.
  • Emitters can become blocked by sediment or mineral deposits.
  • Filters require regular cleaning.
  • Drip lines may be damaged by animals, tools or farm operations.
  • Requires careful installation and maintenance.
  • Water distribution may become uneven when pressure is poorly managed.
Farmer's tip: Check emitters regularly. If some plants receive less water than others, inspect the filters, pipes, valves and emitters before increasing the irrigation duration.

08. Subsurface Irrigation

Subsurface irrigation supplies water below the soil surface. It may use buried perforated pipes, porous materials or specially designed subsurface drip lines.

Water moves through the soil towards plant roots. The system is designed to maintain suitable moisture in the root zone.

How It Works

  1. Water enters an underground distribution system.
  2. It moves through buried pipes or irrigation lines.
  3. Water is released below the soil surface.
  4. Soil moisture spreads around the root zone.
  5. Plant roots absorb the available water.

Advantages

  • Reduces direct exposure of irrigation water to sunlight and wind.
  • Can reduce surface evaporation.
  • Does not wet the entire soil surface.
  • Can support efficient water delivery in suitable soils.
  • May reduce interference with some farm operations.

Disadvantages

  • Installation can be expensive.
  • Underground blockages may be difficult to detect.
  • Leaks can be difficult to locate.
  • Requires careful system design and monitoring.
  • May not be suitable for every soil or crop.
Important: Subsurface irrigation needs appropriate soil assessment. Soil texture, salinity, drainage and root depth affect how water moves and whether the system will perform well.

09. Manual Irrigation

Manual irrigation involves applying water using containers, watering cans, buckets, hoses or other handheld equipment.

Common Manual Irrigation Methods

A. Watering Can

A watering can is used to apply water to individual plants, nursery beds and small gardens.

B. Bucket Irrigation

Farmers carry water in buckets and apply it directly to plants or prepared basins.

C. Hose Irrigation

A hose delivers water from a tank, tap or pump. Water can be directed towards selected crop areas.

D. Manual Basin Watering

Water is poured into small basins constructed around plants, especially individual trees.

Advantages

  • Simple to understand and operate.
  • Requires little specialised equipment.
  • Suitable for small gardens and nurseries.
  • Allows farmers to control water application to individual plants.

Disadvantages

  • Requires considerable labour.
  • Can be time-consuming on large farms.
  • Water application may be uneven.
  • Water transport can be difficult where sources are far away.
  • May become physically demanding.

10. Comparison of Irrigation Methods

Different irrigation methods have different equipment requirements, costs, water distribution patterns and maintenance needs.

Method Water Delivery Typical Application Main Limitation
Surface Flows over soil Suitable field crops and prepared fields Runoff and uneven distribution
Sprinkler Sprayed through nozzles Vegetables, field crops and suitable pastures Wind and evaporation losses
Drip Released near plant roots Vegetables, orchards and row crops Emitter blockage and installation cost

Remember: No single irrigation method is best for every farm. The appropriate method depends on the crop, soil, water supply, land shape, climate, available energy, investment and management skills.

11. How to Select the Right Irrigation System

Before purchasing or installing an irrigation system, farmers should evaluate their farm conditions and production goals.

A. Crop Type

Different crops have different water requirements and root structures. Vegetables, fruit trees, cereals and pasture may require different irrigation arrangements.

B. Soil Type

  • Sandy soil: Water drains quickly, so smaller and more frequent applications may be appropriate.
  • Clay soil: Water moves more slowly, increasing the risk of waterlogging if applied too quickly.
  • Loam soil: Often provides a balance between drainage and water retention.

C. Water Availability

A system must match the amount of water reliably available throughout the growing season.

D. Land Slope and Topography

Land slope affects water movement. Steep or uneven fields may require special design to reduce erosion and improve water distribution.

E. Financial Resources

Farmers should consider the total cost of equipment, installation, energy, repairs, replacement parts and labour.

F. Energy Availability

Pumped systems require a dependable energy source. Options may include electricity, solar energy or fuel-powered pumps.

G. Technical Skills and Maintenance

Farmers should select a system they can operate, inspect and maintain, or arrange reliable technical support where necessary.

H. Water Quality

Water containing sediment, salts or other contaminants may require treatment, filtration or a different irrigation arrangement.

General Selection Guide

12. Irrigation System Installation and Maintenance

Before Installation

  1. Assess the farm size and layout.
  2. Identify and assess the water source.
  3. Test water quality where necessary.
  4. Determine crop water requirements.
  5. Assess soil type, slope and drainage.
  6. Calculate the water supply and system capacity.
  7. Choose suitable pipes, fittings and irrigation equipment.
  8. Prepare a system layout and installation plan.

Basic Drip System Installation

  1. Prepare a stable water storage or supply point.
  2. Install a suitable pump or gravity supply arrangement.
  3. Connect the main supply pipe.
  4. Install a suitable filtration unit.
  5. Connect the sub-main pipes.
  6. Install drip lines along the crop rows.
  7. Fit emitters, valves and connectors as required.
  8. Flush the pipes before normal operation.
  9. Test the system for leaks and even water delivery.

Routine Maintenance

  • Clean filters according to the manufacturer's instructions.
  • Inspect pipes and connectors for leaks.
  • Check pressure and flow rates.
  • Flush irrigation lines where necessary.
  • Replace damaged hoses and fittings.
  • Remove sediment from storage tanks when appropriate.
  • Check pumps, valves and electrical connections safely.
  • Protect equipment from animals, sunlight and physical damage.
  • Keep maintenance records.
Installation advice: The design of a large or pressurised irrigation system should be checked by a qualified irrigation technician or agricultural water specialist. Incorrect pump sizing and pipe design can cause poor performance and equipment damage.

13. Irrigation Scheduling and Crop Water Requirements

Irrigation scheduling means deciding when to irrigate, how much water to apply and how frequently to water crops.

The aim is to maintain sufficient moisture in the root zone without overwatering or allowing crops to experience damaging water stress.

Factors Affecting Irrigation Scheduling

  • Crop type and variety.
  • Stage of crop growth.
  • Soil texture and water-holding capacity.
  • Temperature and solar radiation.
  • Wind speed and humidity.
  • Rainfall received.
  • Root depth.
  • Water availability and irrigation system capacity.

Crop Growth Stages and Water Needs

Growth Stage Importance of Water
Germination Water supports seed swelling and germination.
Seedling stage Supports early root development and establishment.
Vegetative growth Supports leaf, stem and root development.
Flowering Water stress can affect flower development and pollination.
Fruit or grain development Adequate water supports produce development and filling.
Maturity Water management should match crop and harvesting requirements.

Signs of Water Stress

  • Wilting leaves.
  • Leaf curling or rolling.
  • Dry soil around the root zone.
  • Slow plant growth.
  • Premature flower or fruit drop.
  • Reduced crop development.

These symptoms can also be caused by disease, root damage, salinity or other conditions. Check the soil and the plant before increasing irrigation.

Ways to Determine When to Irrigate

1. Soil Inspection

Farmers can examine soil moisture at different depths within the crop root zone. Surface appearance alone may not accurately represent moisture deeper in the soil.

2. Soil Moisture Sensors

Electronic sensors estimate soil moisture and help farmers decide when irrigation is needed.

3. Weather Information

Rainfall, temperature, humidity and other weather information help farmers plan irrigation.

4. Crop Observation

Farmers should monitor crop appearance and growth, while avoiding reliance on wilting as the only indication that irrigation is needed.

Important: Irrigation frequency and quantity should be based on crop requirements, soil conditions and weather. A fixed watering schedule may not be suitable for every farm or season.

14. Water Quality and Water Conservation

A. Water Quality

Water quality is important because unsuitable water can damage crops, affect soil health and block irrigation equipment.

Common Water Quality Concerns

  • Salinity: High dissolved salt levels can affect plant water uptake.
  • Sodicity: Excess sodium can damage soil structure and reduce infiltration.
  • Sediment: Sand and suspended particles can block emitters and nozzles.
  • Iron and mineral deposits: Some minerals can accumulate and block equipment.
  • Biological contamination: Unsafe microorganisms may create health risks.

B. Water Conservation Methods

  • Use drip irrigation where appropriate.
  • Repair leaking pipes and fittings.
  • Apply water according to crop needs.
  • Harvest and store rainwater where feasible.
  • Use mulch to reduce soil surface evaporation.
  • Maintain good soil structure and organic matter.
  • Control weeds that compete for water.
  • Use suitable irrigation times to reduce avoidable losses.
  • Maintain drainage to prevent waterlogging.
  • Monitor water use and keep records.

C. Mulching and Soil Moisture

Mulching involves covering the soil surface with suitable materials such as dry grass, crop residues or approved mulch materials.

Mulching can reduce evaporation, suppress weeds, moderate soil temperature and improve soil protection. Keep mulch appropriately positioned to avoid creating pest or disease problems around plant stems.

Farmer's practice: Water conservation begins with good soil management. Healthy soil with suitable organic matter and structure can help retain water and support crop growth.

15. Digital Technologies in Irrigation

Digital agriculture provides tools for monitoring water availability, soil moisture, weather conditions, irrigation equipment and water use.

A. Soil Moisture Sensors

These devices measure or estimate soil moisture. Readings can help farmers decide when irrigation is necessary.

B. Automated Irrigation Controllers

Controllers operate valves and pumps according to programmed schedules or sensor information.

C. Weather Monitoring Applications

Weather applications provide rainfall forecasts, temperature information and other data that can support irrigation planning.

D. GPS and Digital Farm Mapping

GPS can help map irrigation sections, record water infrastructure and identify areas with uneven crop growth or irrigation coverage.

E. Solar-Powered Irrigation

Solar-powered systems use solar panels to supply electricity to pumps. They can reduce dependence on fuel or grid electricity where conditions are suitable.

F. Remote Monitoring

Some systems allow farmers to monitor pumps, tanks, valves and moisture readings through connected mobile devices.

Digital Irrigation Tools

16. Common Irrigation Challenges and Solutions

Challenge Possible Cause Recommended Response
Insufficient water supply Low source capacity or drought Improve water storage, conserve water and plan production according to availability.
Blocked drip emitters Sediment, algae or mineral deposits Inspect filters, clean lines and follow manufacturer maintenance instructions.
Uneven irrigation Incorrect pressure, damaged pipes or poor layout Check pressure, flow and system design.
Waterlogging Excessive irrigation or poor drainage Adjust application and improve drainage.
High pumping costs Energy prices or inefficient equipment Assess pump efficiency and consider suitable energy alternatives.
Soil salinity Salt accumulation and inadequate drainage Test soil and water and obtain appropriate soil-management advice.
Pipe leakage Physical damage or poor connections Repair or replace damaged sections.
Crop wilting despite irrigation Root problems, salinity, poor water distribution or disease Inspect soil moisture, roots and system performance before changing the schedule.

17. Practical Farmer Field Activities

Activity 1: Identify Irrigation Methods

  1. Visit a farm that uses irrigation.
  2. Observe how water is transported.
  3. Identify how water reaches the crops.
  4. Determine the irrigation method used.
  5. Record its advantages and limitations.

Activity 2: Inspect Soil Moisture

  1. Select a crop field.
  2. Examine the soil near the crop roots.
  3. Compare moisture at different depths.
  4. Observe the crop condition.
  5. Record whether irrigation may be needed.

Activity 3: Inspect a Drip Irrigation System

  1. Identify the water source.
  2. Locate the filter and main pipe.
  3. Identify drip lines and emitters.
  4. Observe water distribution.
  5. Check for leaks and blocked outlets.
  6. Record any maintenance requirements.

Activity 4: Water Conservation Assessment

  1. Identify where water is being wasted.
  2. Inspect leaking pipes and channels.
  3. Observe areas affected by runoff.
  4. Assess the use of mulch.
  5. Suggest practical improvements.

Field Inspection Checklist

  • Water source identified.
  • Water supply assessed.
  • Crop type recorded.
  • Soil type observed.
  • Irrigation method identified.
  • Water distribution checked.
  • Leaks and blockages inspected.
  • Soil moisture assessed.
  • Drainage conditions checked.
  • Water use recorded.
  • Maintenance needs documented.
